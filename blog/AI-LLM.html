Are Large Language Models "Intelligent"? Text Prediction, Embodiment, and the Road to AGI

Author: Robert Long
Position: Principal Consultant (Statistics & Data Science), The Data Guru Ltd
Intended audience: Clinicians, clinical researchers, data scientists, and anyone trying to interpret current claims about "AI" and "AGI".

1. Introduction: Why This Matters for Medicine and Society

In mainstream media and much of industry, large language models (LLMs) are routinely described as "intelligent" or even exhibiting "sparks of AGI". For clinicians and researchers asked to adopt "AI tools", that framing is deeply misleading.

Technically, current LLMs are self-supervised sequence models that learn to predict the next token (word, sub-word, character) in massive text corpora. They are extraordinarily good at this. But statistical competence on text does not automatically entail:

  • Understanding causality,
  • Grounding concepts in the physical world, or
  • Robust, goal-directed agency in noisy, uncertain environments.

This article makes two main claims:

  1. Calling contemporary LLMs "intelligent" in the same sense as humans obscures critical limitations and invites over-reliance.
  2. The plausible path to anything like "AGI" runs through embodied, multimodal, socially grounded, continual learning rather than simply building ever-bigger text models.

The argument draws on work in computational linguistics (for example, the "stochastic parrots" critique of large models),1 neuroscience of predictive processing,2,3 concept representation in grounded vs non-grounded models,4 research on reasoning limitations in LLMs,5,6 continual learning challenges,7 and macroeconomic analysis of the current "AI bubble".8

2. What LLMs Actually Do: Stochastic Parrots, Not General Reasoners

Formally, an LLM is a parameterised probability model that estimates P(tokent | context) over a vocabulary, where the context is usually the previous tokens in the sequence. Training proceeds by adjusting billions of parameters to minimise prediction error on large text datasets.

Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, and Shmitchell (2021) famously describe such systems as "stochastic parrots" — models that can generate fluent text by exploiting statistical regularities in their training data, but without any intrinsic understanding of meaning, reference, or social responsibility.1 Specifically, they argue that LLMs probabilistically link words and sentences together without considering meaning, operating as systems for "haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms" without reference to actual meaning. Their critique is not that these models are useless, but that:

  • They scale environmental and financial costs dramatically,
  • They amplify the biases of the underlying web-scale data, and
  • They encourage people to treat plausible text as authoritative output.

Recent empirical work supports this scepticism about genuine reasoning. MIT researchers found that LLMs "often struggle to handle more complex problems that require true understanding" and that their reasoning abilities are often overestimated, with performance heavily dependent on whether tasks are similar to training data.5 Similarly, six Apple researchers concluded that "current LLMs are not capable of genuine logical reasoning; instead, they attempt to replicate the reasoning steps observed in their training data".6

Clinically, these limitations matter. A system that can convincingly phrase a diagnosis, causal claim, or treatment recommendation is not necessarily one that can reliably reason about physiology, competing risks, or treatment interactions. It is optimised to produce likely-sounding text, not true or safe text.

3. The Scaling Story: From Exponential Gains to Diminishing Returns

From roughly 2020–2024, AI progress followed a relatively simple "scaling" narrative: bigger models, more data, more compute ⇒ better performance across many benchmarks.

However, there are now at least three serious constraints:

  1. Flattening scaling laws. Empirically, incremental performance gains on many tasks now require disproportionately large increases in parameters and compute. Industry leaders now acknowledge that simply using more compute and data during pretraining will not produce "some sort of all-knowing digital god".8
  2. Data exhaustion. High-quality, web-scale text data is effectively "mined out"; further gains require increasingly synthetic or lower-quality data. The scaling laws that powered progress have hit diminishing returns, with high-quality data sources essentially exhausted.8
  3. Macroeconomic limits. Storm (2025) argues that current AI investment represents a speculative bubble: trillions of dollars of planned data-centre capex rest on productivity assumptions that existing LLMs have not come close to delivering.8

In other words, simply building "GPT-7 but bigger" is running into diminishing marginal returns — both technically and economically. If scaling text prediction were enough to reach AGI, we would not see this pattern; we would simply keep turning the "bigger" dial. The evidence suggests we have reached "peak GenAI" in terms of current LLMs, with scaling unable to take us further toward artificial general intelligence as returns diminish rapidly.8

4. The Brain as a Prediction Machine – But of What?

A separate line of work in neuroscience treats the human brain itself as a prediction engine. In predictive processing and related "free-energy" frameworks, perception, action, and cognition arise from hierarchical brains continually generating predictions and minimising prediction error against incoming sensory data (for example, Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013).2,3

Crucially, however, the brain is not predicting tokens. It is predicting:

  • Visual features and trajectories of objects,
  • Motor commands and proprioceptive feedback,
  • Interoceptive signals (heart rate, dyspnoea, nausea),
  • Social signals (facial expressions, tone of voice, posture), and
  • Higher-level states (intentions, threats, rewards).

A recent large meta-analysis by Costa and colleagues (2024) integrates functional MRI data across multiple cognitive domains and finds evidence for a domain-general "Dynamic Prediction Network": a distributed set of regions, including anterior insula, frontal and temporal gyri, and parietal lobules, activated across prediction tasks in cognitive control, language, motor, attention, and social cognition.4 This supports the idea that prediction is a general organising principle of human cognition, not just a quirk of language.

So a fair characterisation might be: humans are also prediction machines — but we are predicting the world, not just words.

5. How LLMs Diverge from Human Concept Representations

A natural counterargument is: "If humans are predictive systems and LLMs are predictive systems, perhaps scaling LLMs plus better training data will eventually give us human-like intelligence."

Recent work by Xu and colleagues (2025) suggests this is overly optimistic. They compared representations of 4,442 concepts across:

  • Human behavioural norms (large-scale ratings of conceptual properties), and
  • State-of-the-art LLMs trained with and without visual input.

They found that:

  • LLM–human similarity is highest for non-sensorimotor features (for example, abstract, social, or affective aspects),
  • Similarity drops markedly for sensory dimensions (for example, visual, auditory, tactile properties), and
  • It is lowest for motor features (for example, actions, bodily movements).9

Notably, LLMs augmented with visual training show improved alignment in vision-related dimensions, but still diverge strongly on motor features.9 Taken together, this suggests that text alone is not enough to approximate human conceptual structure, especially for embodied and action-related concepts.

This is precisely what one would expect if:

  • Human brains learn concepts through multisensory, embodied interaction with the world, and
  • LLMs learn only from linguistic co-occurrence statistics and, at best, paired text–image data.

The so-called symbol grounding problem10 captures this issue: LLMs define symbols in terms of which other symbols they co-occur with, creating potential infinite regress without sensorimotor grounding in the physical world.

6. Why "Just Better Training Data" Is Not a Magic Fix

It is tempting to assume that if we could somehow:

  • Collect higher-quality text,
  • Add more diverse modalities (images, audio, video, physiological traces), and
  • Train on all of this with enough compute,

then an LLM-like architecture would eventually converge to human-level intelligence. But two further obstacles complicate this story:

  1. Embodiment and closed-loop interaction. Brains do not merely passively receive data; they act in the world, generating the very sensory data they then learn from. This tight perception–action loop matters for learning causality and control. Human cognition is grounded in sensorimotor experiences in the external world and subjective feelings in the internal world, with natural languages grounded on shared sensorimotor experiences and feelings.10
  2. Continual learning without catastrophic forgetting. Biological systems update their internal models continuously over years and decades. Humans and animals have the ability to continually acquire, fine-tune, and transfer knowledge and skills throughout their lifespan through a rich set of neurocognitive mechanisms.7 Current large models, by contrast, are typically trained in large offline batches and then either frozen or fine-tuned, with the continual acquisition of incrementally available information from non-stationary data distributions generally leading to catastrophic forgetting.7 Biological mechanisms leverage dual representation of specific and generalized memories within corticohippocampal circuits to facilitate lifelong learning, a capability that current artificial systems significantly lack.11

Research on multi-agent reinforcement learning already shows emergent communication, coordination, and social conventions in relatively simple environments,12 with social learning enabling agents to discover complex skills not learned from single-agent training and adapt online to novel environments by taking cues from experts.13 But these systems remain fragile, task-specific, and heavily engineered. They are a hint of what may be required, not a finished solution.

7. A More Plausible Path Toward General Intelligence

If we take the neuroscience and concept-representation data seriously, a more plausible route towards AGI looks something like this:

  1. Embodied agents.
    Systems that learn in closed-loop interaction with physical or high-fidelity simulated environments, where motor actions have consequences and prediction errors are grounded in real dynamics, not just text inconsistencies. Current research shows that embodied AI is increasingly recognized as a promising path to achieve AGI.14
  2. Multimodal grounding.
    Jointly learning from vision, audition, touch, proprioception, and interoception, so that "heart failure", "breathlessness", or "angina" are tied to real physiological patterns and patient trajectories, not just phrases in guidelines. Symbol grounding, embodiment, causality, and memory represent foundational principles for achieving general intelligence that LLMs currently lack or implement inadequately.10
  3. Social and cultural learning.
    Acquiring norms, roles, and practices via interaction with other agents (human and artificial), including teaching, collaboration, and role-modelling — not just "reading" decontextualised text. Multi-agent systems can demonstrate emergent social learning and cultural transmission,12,13 capabilities essential for human-like intelligence.
  4. Robust continual learning.
    Architectures that can integrate new experience over time without erasing what has already been learned, more akin to human memory systems.7,11
  5. Explicit uncertainty and causal modelling.
    Representing and acting under uncertainty, with explicit causal models where appropriate, rather than relying purely on pattern-matching in token space. LLMs lack an internal "world model" to predict world states and simulate long-term outcomes of actions, preventing them from performing deliberate planning like human brains.15

LLMs may well remain central components in such systems: as language interfaces, world-knowledge priors, code generators, and communication layers. But on this view, they are modules inside a broader architecture, not stand-alone artificial minds.

8. So, Are LLMs "Intelligent"?

In a weak, engineering sense — the ability to perform complex tasks that previously required humans — LLMs undeniably look "intelligent". They can summarise articles, draft code, translate languages, and assist with documentation at near-human levels on many benchmarks.

But if by "intelligent" we mean:

  • Grounded understanding of the physical and social world,
  • Robust causal reasoning and counterfactual thinking,
  • Goal-directed behaviour in open-ended environments, and
  • Lifelong learning from embodied experience,

then current text-trained LLMs simply do not qualify. They are powerful statistical tools, not general agents.

This distinction matters for:

  • Clinical safety (how we validate and monitor "AI" recommendations),
  • Regulation (what level of oversight is appropriate), and
  • Economic planning (how much we should invest, and on what assumptions).

On the evidence we have, scaling text-only prediction is unlikely to get us to AGI. Future breakthroughs, if they arrive, will almost certainly involve embodied, multimodal, socially grounded, continually learning systems — in which LLMs are one important part, but not the whole story.
